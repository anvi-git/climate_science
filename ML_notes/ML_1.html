What is machine learning

To extract information and identify patterns — also called statistical learning.

We assume there's a true underlying relationship between an output and a set of inputs, plus some noise we can't account for:

\( Y = f(X_1, \dots, X_p) + \varepsilon \)
Why do we think this works? We assume f exists. The noise term is assumed well-behaved: \(E(\varepsilon)=0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2\).

Variables

SymbolAlso called
Xinput variables · covariates · predictors · features
Youtput · response · target · label

Two tasks: prediction and inference

Prediction

We have X, but not Y. Treating f as a black box, we just want a usable estimate:

\( \hat{Y} = \hat{f}(X) \)

\(\hat{f}\) is our estimate of f; \(\hat{Y}\) is the resulting prediction for Y.

Inference

Rather than getting good accuracy on \(\hat{Y}\), we want to understand the relationship between X and Y — here f must be an explainable model. Typical questions:

  • Which predictors are important?
  • Which predictors have a positive / negative relation with Y?
  • Is the relation "simple" (e.g. linear) or not?

How accurate is Ŷ to Y?

The error splits into two parts:

  • Reducible error — depends on the model \(\hat{f}\); in principle we can reduce this by improving the model.
  • Irreducible error — depends on \(\varepsilon\), which we cannot measure.
\( E(Y-\hat{Y})^2 = E[f(X)+\varepsilon-\hat{f}(X)]^2 = E\big[(f(X)-\hat{f}(X))^2\big] + \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) \)

reducible error  +  irreducible error

Derivation note Expanding the square, the cross term \(2\,\varepsilon\,[f(X)-\hat f(X)]\) vanishes in expectation since \(E(\varepsilon)=0\), and \(E(\varepsilon^2)=\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)\).

Estimation of f̂

Given m data points — the training data — with predictors \(x_1,\dots,x_m\) and labels \(y_1,\dots,y_m\), we want to find \(\hat f\) such that \(Y \approx \hat f(X)\) for every pair \((X,Y)_{i=1}^{m}\).

Parametric methods

Assume a shape for f (the simplest: linear). The chosen model has \(p+1\) parameters \(\beta_0,\dots,\beta_p\); estimating f becomes finding those parameters — for a linear model, typically by least squares.

Non-parametric methods

No assumption on the shape of f: it's estimated directly from the data points. Needs a lot of data, and brings the risk of overfitting.

Flexibility trade-off Jumping from estimating f to estimating \(\beta_0,\dots,\beta_p\) introduces a distance between \(\hat f\) and f. Reducing that distance means choosing more flexible models — i.e. more parameters — at the cost of adapting too closely to the data we happen to have.

Which approach to prefer?

It depends on the question being asked:

  • Inference — a simple model (few parameters, simple shape) is preferable: it highlights the importance of individual predictors.
  • Prediction — a good algorithm, very accurate, is preferable even if it leaves the model a "black box".

How good is a model? MSE and the U-shape

For regression, we use the Mean Squared Error:

\( \mathrm{MSE} = \dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\big)^2 \)

we want MSE small

Computed on the labeled subset used to fit the model — the training dataset — this gives the training MSE. But the goal is to fit well not the training data, rather future, unseen data points: we need the model for which the average of \((y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2\) over new points is smallest.

low flexibility

Few degrees of freedom. Risk: underfitting — the model can't capture the true shape.

high flexibility

High degrees of freedom. Risk: overfitting — lowest training MSE, but it just replicates the training points and generalizes badly to new ones.

Fundamental property For every dataset, and for every statistical learning method, the test MSE curve is U-shaped as a function of flexibility. The point of minimum test MSE is not the point of minimum training MSE — the most flexible model minimizes training MSE (it fits every training point) but tends to have the highest test MSE.

Supervised vs. unsupervised learning

SettingWhat we haveWhat we can do
Supervised A response \(y_i\) for each \(x_i\) Look for the relation between X and Y, and perform prediction.
Unsupervised The predictors, but no response \(y_i\) Look for relations between different \(X_i, X_j\) — e.g. cluster analysis.

There's also semi-supervised learning, in between the two.

Regression vs. classification

  • Quantitative response → regression problem
  • Qualitative response → classification problem

The focus is on the response; the nature of the predictors matters less.
